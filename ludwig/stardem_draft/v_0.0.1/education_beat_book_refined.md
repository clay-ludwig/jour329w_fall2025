# Education Beat Book - Easton Star-Democrat
## Maryland's Eastern Shore

This guide covers the education beat for Maryland's Eastern Shore, focusing on the school districts, institutions, and key players that shape local education coverage. It is a living document that will evolve as coverage deepens and new information emerges.

---

## The Eastern Shore Education Landscape

Maryland's Eastern Shore encompasses six public school districts—Dorchester, Talbot, Wicomico, Somerset, Caroline, and Kent—each operating independently but facing similar demographic and economic pressures common to rural and semi-rural areas of the Mid-Atlantic. Understanding how these districts connect through state policy, regional challenges, and shared policy debates is essential to covering education on the Shore.

As of November 2025, several major story threads are visible. Dorchester County Public Schools is grappling with a bottom-tier ranking among Maryland districts and has articulated an explicit goal to improve. Talbot County is experiencing significant school-board political activity tied to national culture-war issues, particularly around curriculum and library materials. At Kent County High School, a 50-year-old student radio station faces infrastructure challenges that reflect broader questions about how districts sustain legacy extracurricular programs. Washington College, located in Chestertown, hosts a student-run civic-dialogue group that models civil discourse on polarized topics. Caroline County has promoted internal talent into leadership positions and celebrates student achievement through visible recognition programs, suggesting different governance approaches across the Shore. Beyond these visible threads, systematic reporting on Wicomico and Somerset will be necessary to prevent the beat from becoming Dorchester-and-Talbot-centric simply because those are where early stories landed.

## Dorchester County: The Turnaround Story

Dorchester County Public Schools is ranked 23rd of 23 Maryland counties in educational performance, a distinction that has become a focal point for the district's leadership and board. Superintendent Jymil Thompson and the board have stated an explicit goal to climb to at least 15th place, making this a concrete, measurable objective that can anchor ongoing coverage.

The district underwent significant board turnover in July 2024. Board President Mike Diaz and Vice President Susan Morgan both resigned prior to that date. The Dorchester County Council appointed two new members—Brandy Dawson Cumberland to District 1 and Christine LaMonica to District 3—on July 21, 2024. The newly constituted board includes President Sherri Hubbard, Vice President Talibah Chikwendu, and continuing member Chris Wheedleton. Chikwendu expressed optimism about having a full board after the recent vacancies.

This turnover creates several reporting opportunities. The first is straightforward accountability: what specific strategies is the board pursuing to improve the district's ranking? Are there new curriculum initiatives, staffing changes, or budget reallocations? The second is more investigative: why did Diaz and Morgan resign, and did their departures reflect political pressure, personal circumstances, or disagreement over district direction? The third is longitudinal: tracking whether the board's stated goal translates into measurable progress over the coming years.

Beyond rankings, the human dimension matters. How are teachers in a low-ranking district managing morale? What do students and families think about their schools? How does a ranking like "23rd of 23" affect community perception and enrollment decisions? These stories should be woven into coverage as the year progresses. A longitudinal series tracking Dorchester's improvement trajectory would measure progress against the stated goal, capture the lived experience of students and staff, and provide accountability journalism. Benchmark data collection should begin now to establish baselines for future comparison. Comparative analysis across the Shore—looking at graduation rates, test scores, per-pupil spending, and demographic factors—could provide context for why some districts outperform others and identify specific leverage points for improvement.

## Talbot County: Politics, Curriculum Battles, and Teacher Excellence

Talbot County Public Schools, led by Superintendent Sharon Pepukayi, is experiencing active school-board political engagement centered on curriculum and library policy. The 2024 election cycle has drawn significant involvement from the local Moms for Liberty chapter, an activist parent organization that the Southern Poverty Law Center identifies as extremist.

Candidates linked to M4L as of October 2024 include Ann O'Connor, Karla Wieland-Cherry, and Kerry Gunshenan. Editorial endorsements have gone to candidates identified as opposing the M4L agenda: Candace Henry, Anna Howie, and Cynthia Marrah Jurrius. The specific issues driving this political activity include book challenges and concerns about curriculum standards that address slavery and racial history. One particular flashpoint is a curriculum standard that reportedly teaches that slavery "was beneficial to Black Americans"—a framing that has drawn sharp criticism. The origin, adoption process, and actual language of this standard merit investigation. Is it a Maryland Department of Education standard, a district-level policy, or something else? Has it been formally adopted, and if so, how are teachers implementing it? What do historians and educators say about its accuracy?

The M4L influence in Talbot is part of a broader national pattern, but it deserves local reporting that goes beyond national templates. What specific books have been challenged? How have teachers responded to challenges? What do community members—particularly Black residents and families—think about the direction of these debates? An investigation into the curriculum standard about slavery would serve both Talbot and potentially other Shore districts. This requires document review, interviews with educators and historians, and community input, particularly from Black families and community leaders. A comparison of school-board election dynamics across the Shore would examine where activist groups like M4L are active, how successful they have been, and what policies have resulted from their involvement. An interactive tracker of candidates, endorsements, and key issues could provide readers with a comprehensive view of political activity in education across all six counties.

Beyond the political turbulence, Talbot County also offers a counterbalance to the culture-war narrative. Kelly Coutts, a reading specialist at St. Michaels Elementary, received a Fulbright Teacher Exchange award for the 2024-2025 school year—one of only 23 elementary teachers nationwide to receive the honor. The Talbot Board of Education recognized Coutts at its July 2024 meeting. This achievement highlights the caliber of educators on the Eastern Shore and provides a hook to explore how international experiences can enrich local classrooms. A profile of Coutts's Fulbright journey, her research focus, and how she plans to bring global perspectives back to St. Michaels would offer readers a story about professional excellence and global collaboration. Broader reporting on how TCPS identifies, supports, and celebrates teachers who achieve national honors could examine the district's commitment to educator development and whether Fulbright alumni from the Eastern Shore are forming networks that could benefit neighboring districts.

Talbot County's performance data also provides important context for the broader beat. In October 2023, TCPS announced that the Class of 2023 achieved a 97.7 percent graduation rate with a 0.57 percent dropout rate. Fifty-four percent of seniors completed at least one Advanced Placement course, while 27 percent earned dual-enrollment credit and 42 percent earned a CTE completer credential. Graduates secured 13.9 million dollars in scholarships, including 582,000 dollars in local awards. The composite SAT score was 941. These figures provide a significant benchmark for neighboring districts and a point of comparison in any "ranking-up" narrative for Dorchester. A story examining what Talbot is doing right—particularly around CTE enrollment, dual-enrollment partnerships, and AP participation—could serve as a model for improvement elsewhere on the Shore.

## Caroline County: Internal Promotion and Leadership Development

Caroline County Public Schools, led by Superintendent Dr. Derek Simmons, offers a different governance model worth monitoring. In February 2024, the district announced the appointment of Chastity Wright as Assistant Principal at North Caroline High School. Wright's career trajectory—from reading-intervention teacher to dean of students to assistant principal—illustrates an internal talent pipeline that some districts cultivate and others overlook. Her background in PBIS (Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports) and UDL (Universal Design for Learning) suggests a focus on equitable school climate and inclusive instruction.

This promotion model differs from the board turnover seen in Dorchester and raises questions about governance stability and institutional knowledge. When districts promote from within, do policies show greater continuity? Do staff morale and retention improve? Comparing internal promotion practices across Eastern Shore districts could reveal different approaches to leadership development and their effects on school climate and student outcomes.

Caroline County also demonstrates a commitment to recognizing student achievement. In December 2023, the district held a board presentation celebrating Summer Smith, a Colonel Richardson High School student whose artwork was selected for the district's holiday card. The presentation included Art Teacher Melanie Bolles, Principal Jared Sherman, and Supervisor of Instruction Renee Hesson, signaling that the district makes student recognition a visible, board-level priority. This practice of elevating student work to formal settings—rather than confining it to classroom walls—offers a model for how districts build student confidence and community pride. Following the district's annual holiday-card contest, profiling the winning students, and examining how the district celebrates visual-arts achievement could reveal the infrastructure for student recognition and its effects on arts enrollment and school culture.

## Kent County: Sustaining Legacy Programs Through Transition

Kent County Public Schools is navigating a superintendent transition at a critical moment for one of its flagship extracurricular programs. Dr. Karen Couch, the outgoing superintendent, championed the student-run radio station WKHS 90.5 FM at Kent County High School, which celebrated its 50-year anniversary in March 2024. The station represents a significant component of the school's Career and Technical Education program and has served as a pipeline for students interested in media careers.

WKHS has undergone substantial infrastructure investment in recent years. In 2018, the district funded a 160,000-dollar upgrade that created six new studios, a modernized newsroom, and expanded production spaces. Station manager Chris Singleton has led the program since 2008, with part-time support from Ken Collins on sales and fundraising. The station broadcasts at 90.5 FM and maintains a website at wkhsradio.org. However, the program now faces a pressing infrastructure challenge: the original 1973 transmission tower is aging and requires replacement. Securing funding for this capital project will be a test of the incoming superintendent's commitment to CTE and extracurricular programming.

The tower replacement represents a broader challenge facing rural school districts: how to maintain long-standing, technology-intensive programs amid shifting media consumption habits, budget pressures, and leadership transitions. The station's sustainability depends not just on funding but on district leadership that understands its educational value. As Dr. Couch retires and a new superintendent takes office, monitoring the district's stance on this project will reveal priorities around career and technical education. The timeline and financing plan for the tower replacement merit close attention, as do any safety or FCC compliance issues that might accelerate the project.

Reporting on WKHS also offers opportunities to examine student outcomes. Following alumni to see how their radio experience translates into college majors or media careers would demonstrate the program's long-term impact. The station's role in emergency broadcasting for Kent County and its community partnerships with local advertisers are also worth exploring. More broadly, examining how districts across the Shore maintain legacy programs and invest in CTE infrastructure during budget pressures could reveal competing priorities and long-term planning capacity.

## Washington College: Student Civic Engagement and Community Dialogue

Washington College in Chestertown, led by President Dr. Laura McGowan, hosts several education-related story opportunities that extend beyond traditional K-12 coverage. The college's student-run political-discussion group, PoliTalks, has become a model for civil discourse on polarized topics. Founded by seniors Stephen Hook and Zach Affeldt, the club provides what organizers describe as a "safe, respectful forum for political education and conversation." In May 2025, leadership transitioned to co-presidents Charlotte Schurman and Grace Walsh.

The club's public-lecture model has attracted local officials and community members. Mayor David Foster and Council Member Sheila Austrian of Chestertown attended spring lectures, and the club has hosted speakers including Heather Mizeur, a former Maryland delegate and CEO of WeAreOne Alliance. Upcoming programming includes an environmental-justice lecture with Maryland Secretary of the Environment Serena C. McIlwain.

PoliTalks exemplifies how college-level extracurricular groups can model civil discourse, influence community dialogue, and serve as a pipeline for future civic leaders. Profiling Schurman and Walsh and their vision for the group would capture the leadership transition and any shifts in programming. Examining PoliTalks' approach to free speech and polarized politics—and comparing it to recent campus controversies at other Maryland colleges—could assess whether student-run forums effectively mitigate polarization. Documenting how local officials and residents engage with the club, and whether the public lectures influence broader Easton-area civic conversation, would connect higher education to the wider community. The model of town-college collaboration that PoliTalks exemplifies merits investigation across the Shore, particularly where Washington College and local government interact on education policy.

## Community Adult Education and Lifelong Learning

Beyond K-12 and higher education, the Eastern Shore supports a robust adult-learning ecosystem that complements formal schooling. Chesapeake Forum, a nonprofit that recently merged with the Easton Family YMCA, offers a broad slate of classes and lectures that address workforce development, cultural enrichment, and community engagement. The organization's spring 2024 lineup included more than 30 offerings—from basic car maintenance and birding to civil-war history and ancient Greek drama—reflecting the diversity of learner interests and needs on the Shore.

In April 2024, Chesapeake Forum held a preview party at the Academy Art Museum in Easton to unveil its spring class offerings. The partnership with the museum signals how community-education providers leverage cultural institutions to build visibility and community connection. The forum's "Lectures on the Shore" speaker series has featured programming with regional and national relevance, including a May 2024 event titled "Lights, Camera, Action" that chronicled the Fine family's 40-year documentary filmmaking legacy, including their Oscar-winning film Inocente.

Reporting on adult education merits attention because it reveals how communities sustain learning and skill development across the lifespan. Profiles of participants who have leveraged Chesapeake Forum classes into new jobs, career changes, or community projects would demonstrate the program's impact on workforce development. Investigating how the YMCA merger has altered funding, outreach, and facility usage could reveal how public-private partnerships shape educational access. The Academy Art Museum's role as a hub for community-education events and potential partnerships with K-12 art curricula also warrants exploration.

## Emerging Themes and Story Opportunities

Five major themes are taking shape, each with distinct reporting angles and long-term potential.

**District Performance and Accountability.** Dorchester's stated goal to improve from 23rd to 15th place offers a concrete framework for ongoing coverage. A longitudinal series could track the board's strategic initiatives, examine whether new policies correlate with measurable gains, and explore the lived experience of students and teachers within a low-ranking system. Comparative analysis across the Shore—looking at graduation rates, test scores, per-pupil spending, and demographic factors—could provide context for why some districts outperform others. Talbot County's strong performance data (97.7 percent graduation rate, 13.9 million dollars in scholarships, 941 composite SAT score) provides a benchmark for this comparison. This reporting should include data deep-dives into benchmark metrics, policy audits of any new initiatives launched after the July 2024 board changes, and human-interest angles capturing how teachers and students navigate a system working toward improvement.

**School-Board Politics and Culture Wars.** The M4L activity in Talbot signals that national debates about curriculum, library materials, and "inclusive" education have reached the Shore. Coverage should map where these activist groups are active across all counties, track their electoral success, and examine the actual policy changes they seek or achieve. This requires both political reporting—who's running, who's winning, what's their platform—and substantive reporting about what specific books are being challenged, how teachers respond to challenges, and how curriculum decisions get made. An election-year tracker for all Eastern Shore counties would note candidate filings, endorsements, fundraising, and key issues. A deeper investigation into the disputed slavery curriculum standard would trace its origin, legislative pathway, and district-level implementation, with input from historians and educators on its accuracy. Moderated discussions with parents, teachers, and activists representing different perspectives on curriculum and library policies could capture the full spectrum of community opinion. These events should be designed to center the voices of those most affected by education policy, particularly Black residents and families.

**Board Turnover and Governance.** Multiple board resignations in Dorchester raise questions about why people leave these positions, what pressures they face, and how turnover affects policy continuity. This is particularly important in a district trying to execute a multi-year improvement plan—does board instability undermine progress? Caroline County's internal promotion model and stable board composition offer a contrasting approach. Comparative analysis of districts that experienced multiple board resignations in recent years versus those with stable governance could reveal patterns in policy continuity and student outcomes. A board-member profile or series could examine who serves on these boards, why they run, what pressures they face, and how turnover affects continuity. Dual profiles of superintendents like Jymil Thompson and Sharon Pepukayi could highlight different leadership styles and visions for district improvement.

**Teacher Excellence and Professional Development.** Kelly Coutts's Fulbright award suggests that the Eastern Shore has educators achieving national recognition. Reporting on how districts identify, support, and celebrate teachers pursuing such honors could reveal the infrastructure for professional growth and whether these achievements translate into broader improvements in teaching and learning. This angle also provides a counternarrative to coverage of political conflict, grounding the beat in educator excellence and global collaboration. Broader reporting on the teacher-excellence pipeline in TCPS could examine how the district supports educators pursuing national honors and whether Fulbright alumni are forming networks to benefit neighboring districts.

**Sustaining Legacy Extracurricular Programs.** WKHS's 50-year history and looming tower replacement illustrate the challenges of maintaining technology-intensive CTE initiatives amid shifting media consumption and budget pressures. Coverage should examine how districts secure funding for capital projects, how leadership transitions affect program continuity, and what happens to students who participate in these programs. Investigating how Kent County's budget process handles extracurricular capital projects, especially during superintendent transitions, will reveal institutional priorities. Following WKHS alumni to see how radio experience translates into college and career paths would demonstrate the program's long-term impact. More broadly, examining how districts across the Shore maintain legacy programs and invest in CTE infrastructure during budget pressures could reveal competing priorities and long-term planning capacity.

**Student Recognition and School Culture.** Caroline County's visible celebration of student achievement—exemplified by the board-level recognition of Summer Smith's artwork—suggests that how districts honor student work affects school culture and community engagement. Reporting on these recognition practices across the Shore could reveal different approaches to student achievement and their effects on enrollment, engagement, and school climate. Profiles of students whose work receives district-level recognition could provide human-interest angles while demonstrating the diversity of student talent on the Shore.

**Community Voice and Racial Equity.** The M4L editorial calls for reporters to "speak with local Black citizens" to capture their perspectives on the extremist agenda. Hosting listening sessions with parents, students, and educators—particularly in communities most affected by policy changes or performance issues—will generate sources, story ideas, and a deeper understanding of how district decisions affect real people. A series of first-person profiles from Black students, parents, and educators discussing how board politics affect their daily school experience would center equity concerns and ground abstract policy debates in lived experience.

The college's student-organization liaison office can facilitate introductions to club leadership and provide information about public events.

Chesapeake Forum can be reached at info@chesapeakeforum.org or through its website at chesapeakeforum.org. The organization's staff can provide information about upcoming classes, speaker series, and partnership opportunities with schools and community institutions.

Building a broader source list—including teachers, parents, students, and community leaders across all six counties—should be an ongoing priority. When reaching out to districts, request a media kit with superintendent contact information and a list of registered candidates for upcoming board elections. Ask the district's staff-development office about existing teacher-excellence programs and recognition systems. For CTE coverage, ask for strategic plans on extracurricular funding and capital-project timelines. For adult education, ask Chesapeake Forum about participant outcomes and partnership opportunities with K-12 districts.

## Using This Beat Book

For daily beat work, monitor scheduled board meetings and superintendent announcements across all six counties. Maintain contact lists and reach out regularly to superintendents and board members for updates on policy changes, staffing, or emerging issues. Scan district calendars for award ceremonies, teacher-recognition events, and other opportunities to capture positive stories about educator excellence. Note upcoming events at Washington College, particularly PoliTalks public lectures, as these often attract local officials and offer community-engagement angles. Follow Chesapeake Forum's class offerings and speaker series for adult-education angles and community-engagement opportunities.

When pitching stories, draw from the emerging themes. Is there progress to report on Dorchester's improvement goal? Are there new developments in the M4L activity in Talbot? Have other Shore districts experienced board turnover or policy changes? What is the status of the Kent County superintendent transition and its implications for WKHS? Are there human-interest angles—teacher profiles, student stories, parent perspectives—that can bring these institutional issues to life?

For investigative work, use the identified story ideas as starting points. Deep reporting on the slavery curriculum standard, for example, would require reviewing policy documents, interviewing educators and historians, analyzing how the standard is being taught, and capturing community reaction. Similarly, a comparison of board elections across the Shore would require systematic data collection and analysis. Investigation into the reasons for Dorchester board resignations would involve interviews with departing members, board colleagues, and community observers.

Community engagement should be built into the beat from the start. Hosting listening sessions with parents, students, and educators—particularly in communities most affected by policy changes or performance issues—will generate sources, story ideas, and a deeper understanding of how district decisions affect real people. Moderated discussions with parents, teachers, and activists representing different perspectives on curriculum and library policies could capture the full spectrum of community opinion. These events should be designed to center the voices of those most affected by education policy, particularly Black residents and families.
