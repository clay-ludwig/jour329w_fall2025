# Education Beat Book – Easton Star-Democrat
## Maryland's Eastern Shore

This guide equips reporters with the people, institutions, issues, and story ideas shaping education coverage on the Eastern Shore. It is updated as new reporting unfolds and should serve as a launchpad for deep-dive reporting, quick-turn pieces, and long-form features.

---

## Understanding the Eastern Shore Education Landscape

The Eastern Shore's education system spans three county school districts—Talbot, Caroline, and Dorchester—plus Chesapeake College, the regional community college serving five Mid-Shore counties. This geography matters for coverage because decisions at the state and county level often intersect, particularly around capital funding, workforce training, and implementation of the Blueprint for Maryland's Future, the state's education reform initiative.

The beat encompasses several interconnected layers of coverage: governance transitions and board dynamics, budget pressures from health-care costs and delayed state funding, technology and discipline policies, mental-health and violence-prevention initiatives, environmental education and hands-on STEM, civic engagement programs, federal immigration policy impacts on school climate, post-pandemic student readiness, alternative educational models, arts programming, workforce development, higher-education initiatives, school facility capital projects, and community partnerships filling gaps left by school budget constraints.

## Board Politics and Governance

Board politics shifted significantly in November 2024. The Talbot County school board election saw three newcomers win seats, reflecting broader concerns about how state education reforms are being implemented locally. Candidates backed by parent-rights organizations saw success in Talbot and other Shore districts. Reporters should investigate whether current board members have ties to parent-advocacy organizations and how their priorities align with Blueprint mandates and support services for vulnerable students.

Dorchester County stabilized its board following dual resignations in June 2024. Board President Mike Diaz and Vice President Susan Morgan both resigned. Brandy Dawson Cumberland and Christine LaMonica were appointed to fill vacated seats. The board is now led by President Sheri Hubbard, with Vice President Talibah Chikwendu. Jymil Thompson was appointed superintendent, officially beginning July 1, 2024.

Caroline County is led by Superintendent Derek Simmons, appointed effective July 1, 2022. In March 2024, board member Donna DiGiacomo resigned following a public clash with Superintendent Simmons over workplace culture issues, raising important questions about superintendent-board relations that reporters should monitor.

## Childhood Literacy and Early-Learning Deficits

Childhood literacy is a significant emerging challenge on the Eastern Shore, though recent data show progress. Students in Dorchester County saw English scores rise from 33.7% to 41.3% in the 2024-25 school year, representing substantial gains. Third-grade reading ability is a national predictor of high school graduation; early gaps compound over a child's academic trajectory.

In response to budget pressures, Dorchester County has pursued an innovative community-partnership model for early childhood development. The Robbins YMCA Reading Hub, operated in partnership with the county schools, public library, United Way of the Lower Eastern Shore, and Operation Pathways, serves as a resource center for early-childhood reading groups for prenatal mothers, infants, toddlers, and preschoolers. This partnership emerged partly out of necessity. Dorchester County Public Schools entered the 2025 budget cycle with a $10 million deficit, prompting cuts to after-school and summer programming. The hub represents a deliberate effort to move beyond traditional school-based tutoring toward a comprehensive, community-embedded approach to early literacy.

Public libraries have become central to this ecosystem. Large-scale book festivals and author visits draw families into public libraries, often in partnership with programs like Dolly Parton's Imagination Library. Open Doors Partners in Education, a nonprofit based in Chestertown serving Kent County and surrounding areas, provides free and low-cost Orton-Gillingham literacy tutoring from pre-K through adult learners. In 2025, the nonprofit experienced a significant setback when fire destroyed its facility and materials, resulting in approximately $40,000 in losses. Despite this crisis, the organization rapidly relocated and is executing a five-year strategic plan for expansion across the Eastern Shore.

## Environmental Education and Hands-On STEM

Environmental education has emerged as a growth area for Shore schools. Two Caroline County elementary schools—Denton Elementary and Greensboro Elementary—earned Maryland Association for Environmental and Outdoor Education Green-School certification in 2025. The certification process required a two-year application and curriculum redesign with partnerships from Pickering Creek Audubon Center, Chesapeake Bay Foundation, and Tuckahoe State Park. Reporters should track whether other Shore schools are pursuing similar certification.

STEM engagement is expanding through workplace partnerships. Kent County High School operates a sophomore STEM job-shadowing program that places students in daylong tours of local businesses. The partnership links classroom learning directly to real-world occupational pathways. Reporters should monitor longitudinal outcomes for participants—college enrollment, apprenticeship uptake, and employer hiring of program graduates—to assess the program's impact.

## Technology, Discipline, and Student Engagement

A significant technology-discipline initiative emerged at Easton Middle School in December 2024. Principal Kelly Murdoch piloted locked-phone pouches (Yondr brand), reporting higher student engagement and fewer multitasking interruptions. The policy has already been adopted by Caroline and Dorchester districts and is under consideration for expansion in Talbot County. Yondr boasts that millions of students from all 50 states are now using its pouches.

In August 2025, Dorchester County's Board of Education approved a district-wide policy that prohibits personal electronic devices for the entire school day. Devices must be turned off and stored; violations can lead to confiscation and discipline. Board President Sheri Hubbard highlighted a high schooler's testimony that phone-free lunches fostered real conversation. The board reviewed 115 public comments before voting.

These device policies have created genuine tensions. Parents are divided: some praise the focus they create; others worry about emergency access. Reporters should track compliance rates across districts, investigate equity implications for students with disabilities or emergency communication needs, and document student and parent perspectives as implementation continues.

Discipline data from Talbot County offers an important baseline for ongoing coverage. As of October 2025, the district reported zero expulsions over the previous three years and steady out-of-school suspension rates, suggesting a focus on restorative-justice approaches.

## Mental-Health Initiatives and Violence Prevention

Mental-health support has emerged as a cross-cutting priority across Shore education. In October 2023, international motivational performer Alex Boyé toured Talbot County Public Schools under the "You Are a 10!" program, delivering talks on self-worth, resilience, and mental-health awareness to students from 5th through 12th grade, funded by the MD Aware II grant.

Violence prevention has become an emerging priority. Dorchester County Public Schools is piloting Risk Threat Crisis' Violence Prevention Initiative at North Dorchester High School, with support from the nonprofit Someone to Walk With. The program trains students, staff, and parents to recognize warning signs and intervene early. Rebecca Redmer, founder of Someone to Walk With and co-creator of the Violence Prevention Initiative, is a key source for understanding community-based approaches to school safety and grant-seeking strategies. Reporters should track the pilot's first-semester outcomes and the program's expansion to other schools and districts.

## Capital Planning and School Facilities

Capital projects are reshaping the Eastern Shore's educational infrastructure. In Caroline County, the Board of Education approved a FY 2027-30 capital-improvement program that earmarks significant funding for a new Lockerman Middle School. The Ed Specs Committee recommended the North Caroline High School property as the preferred site, citing infrastructure benefits and cost savings. The total projected cost is $103.82 million, with roughly $80 million expected from the state and the remainder from local sources. Superintendent Derek Simmons indicated the county's ask to the state will be trimmed after "research of student population and other factors."

The recommendation emphasized several advantages of the North Caroline site: clear paths for indoor and outdoor activities, the absence of street crossings, and the ability to share the high school's large auditorium and gym for events. Development on the North Carolina site would be approximately $500,000 cheaper than the alternative Double Hills Road site. An existing solar array at the site would cost roughly $180,000 to purchase and relocate, though traffic on Central Avenue is a noted community concern that warrants further investigation.

Reporters should track the negotiation process between Caroline County and the Maryland State Department of Education to determine how much of the projected cost will ultimately become a local burden. The demographic trends and enrollment projections that drive capital-needs estimates deserve investigation. Comparing Caroline's capital-improvement timeline with other Eastern Shore districts will reveal regional patterns in school-building funding. Additional story angles include examining how co-locating a middle school with a high school could affect student outcomes and transportation logistics, investigating the feasibility and cost-benefit of acquiring the existing solar array versus installing a new system, and assessing traffic impact on Central Avenue.

In Talbot County, Kevin Shafer, Director of Operations, is overseeing a multi-million-dollar renovation of Chapel District Elementary School. Reporters should track the project timeline, budget, and any construction-related impacts on student instruction.

## Talbot County Public Schools

Talbot County is led by Superintendent Sharon Pepukayi, who has served since July 2022. At the July 16, 2025 board meeting, the Talbot County Board of Education approved a five-year strategic plan and began consolidating retirement-plan providers—a cost-saving measure reflecting broader budget pressures. The board also announced plans for a new staff health center slated to open in October 2025. In May 2025, the district cut summer programming due to grant loss, and notably, the school board was excluded from the decision-making process. This detail raises important questions about superintendent authority, budget decision-making processes, and board oversight.

In January 2025, Superintendent Pepukayi issued a letter to families reaffirming that schools remain "safe places" for all students, regardless of immigration status, after the Trump administration rescinded the 2018 "sensitive locations" policy. Reporters should track whether other Shore districts issue similar communications and investigate whether immigration enforcement activity is affecting enrollment or student attendance.

The Talbot County Education Foundation honored three distinguished alumni in November 2024—Keasha Haythe (Foundation of Hope president and Talbot County Council member), JoAnn "Asparagus" Murray (former family-law magistrate), and Dr. Ludwig Eglseder III (local physician). The ceremony raised funds for six teacher-requested projects, including a student-leadership program, pre-K language and literacy initiative, and Easton Middle School band. Superintendent Pepukayi emphasized the importance of teachers being able to pitch ideas and secure external support. This represents a broader story opportunity: tracking how alumni-driven fundraising translates into concrete programmatic outcomes and examining the role of external funding in supplementing strained school budgets.

## Caroline County Public Schools

Caroline County's arts programming offers a consistent reporting opportunity. The district publishes a detailed concert schedule each December, featuring student performances at venues like North Caroline High School and Colonel Richardson High School. A notable partnership emerged in July 2025 when Ashton Mooday, a local singer-songwriter and music teacher, collaborated with Washington College to give ten Chestertown middle-school students a professional recording experience. The college's audio-engineer students Stevie Lyles and Mariana Kilman operated the studio equipment, allowing the students to record a finished track in a professional setting. Mooday is planning to expand her music instruction facility to accommodate summer camps and homeschooling classes, reflecting growing community demand for expanded arts education. This collaboration illustrates how local arts educators can leverage higher-education resources to deliver high-impact experiential learning and points to an emerging story angle: mapping college-K-12 partnerships across the Eastern Shore and identifying gaps where similar collaborations might be replicated.

## Dorchester County Public Schools

Dorchester County provides a window into post-pandemic student readiness and strategic reform. In 2024, the district reported a 7-percentage-point jump in kindergarten readiness, with students "demonstrating" readiness rising from 32 percent to 39 percent. Language, literacy, and math scores improved, but social-foundation and physical-well-being metrics slipped.

Superintendent Jymil Thompson, who began in July 2024, has launched an ambitious strategic plan aimed at moving the district from 23rd to 15th in state rankings within four years. The plan emphasizes instructional leadership, academic improvement, operational optimization, partnerships, and community engagement. Early results show promise, with the district climbing in state rankings after the first year of implementation.

## The Blueprint for Maryland's Future and Local Implementation

The state's Blueprint for Maryland's Future is reshaping education across the Shore. In 2025, lawmakers approved compromise legislation to trim the Blueprint for Maryland's Future while keeping much of the multiyear plan intact. The Excellence in Maryland Public Schools Act delays implementation of "collaborative time," preserves funding for community schools and protects students in poverty, those in special education and those in classes for non-English speakers from any potential per pupil funding cuts.

The 2024 Talbot County board race offered a clear articulation of local perspectives on the Blueprint. Candidates emphasized concerns about state mandates limiting local flexibility. This critique reflects a broader tension between state-level reform and local control—a theme that appears in board meetings, candidate platforms, and community conversations across all three counties. Reporters should monitor how this tension plays out as the Blueprint rolls forward.

## Emerging Budget and Fiscal Issues

School budgets across the Shore are facing pressure from multiple directions simultaneously. Rising health-care costs, delayed state funding, and capital project obligations are creating a fiscal squeeze that deserves sustained coverage.

Delayed state funding compounds budget pressures. Queen Anne's County's financial audit, initiated in 2024, follows board frustration over state payments that often do not arrive until June, forcing districts to manage cash flow challenges throughout the fiscal year. Reporters should monitor the audit's findings and consider whether similar budget pressures exist in Talbot, Caroline, and Dorchester. The Dorchester County example—a $10 million deficit forcing cuts to summer and after-school programming—shows how budget pressures cascade into reduced services for students most dependent on school-based support.

## Workforce Development and Higher Education

Chesapeake College is pursuing a major capital project: replacing its Queen Anne's Tech building. The state will support 75 percent of project costs, and the counties will support the remaining 25 percent. The proposed new building will take three years to build and will house programs in agriculture, electrical, welding and fabrication, construction trades, and advanced manufacturing.

The college's winter programming—including CDL information sessions, virtual skilled-trades info sessions, and entry-level marine-service-technician and engine-service classes—illustrates how a two-year institution partners with local industry and workforce agencies to create pipelines for students. Reporters should monitor enrollment, completion rates, and employer feedback to gauge the effectiveness of these workforce pathways.

Chesapeake College is actively pursuing Hispanic-Serving Institution (HSI) status, a designation that unlocks federal grant funding. The college has experienced rapid growth in Hispanic enrollment—up 64 percent over the last decade and now representing more than 43 percent of the college's minority students. The college has translated all communications into Spanish, hired Spanish-speaking staff, and partnered with Talbot County Public Schools family liaisons for FAFSA-night community events that achieved a 90 percent application rate. This trajectory demonstrates how demographic shifts are reshaping higher-education priorities on the Shore and creating new grant-seeking opportunities for institutions. Reporters should track the college's HSI application timeline and what federal funding might become available if the designation is granted. Clifford Coppersmith, President of Chesapeake College, is a key source for discussing capital funding, enrollment trends, workforce programming, employer partnerships, and HSI initiatives.

Washington College, located in Chestertown on the Kent County side of the Shore, opened "The Golden Goose" pub in early 2025 inside the former Hynson Lounge. The college applied for a liquor license amendment. Restrictions include "21-plus only," ID checks, wristbands, and a ban on outside alcohol. The pub is not open to the public; students may bring two guests. This development illustrates the intersection of campus-life enhancements, local regulatory oversight, and student-safety concerns. Reporters should monitor compliance checks and any incidents that arise during the first year of operation.

## Community Organizations and Youth Development

Beyond the three county school districts, a network of nonprofit organizations and civic groups shape education and youth development on the Eastern Shore. Queen Anne's County Advocates for Youth, a volunteer-run nonprofit coordinated by Executive Director Judy Corchiarino, coordinates programs and projects for local youth and families.

The U.S. Naval Sea Cadet Corps operates the Maryland Eastern Shore Unit, a youth development program serving ages 10-18. Unit liaison Lt. Maryjoe Phillips oversees a program that teaches naval customs, leadership, discipline, and citizenship. The program has grown from 5 cadets in 2018 to 29 in 2024, demonstrating sustained community interest in military-linked youth development.

The Foundation of HOPE, a nonprofit serving middle-school girls in Talbot County, represents another dimension of community-based youth development. Founded in 2017, the organization runs an after-school program focused on economic development, entrepreneurship, and self-confidence. Executive Director Keasha Haythe is leading an expansion effort that includes construction of a new 5,200-square-foot building in Easton. The project has a total cost of $2.2 million, with $1.8 million already raised.

The Chesapeake Culinary Center, a nonprofit adult-education program based in Denton, Caroline County, teaches cooking, budgeting, meal-prep, and food-preservation skills through free and fee-based workshops. Operations Manager and Head Chef David Murray leads a program that offers classes in English, Spanish, and Haitian Creole and has plans to expand to other Eastern Shore counties.

## Chronic Absenteeism and Student Attendance

Chronic absenteeism has emerged as a statewide policy focus, and Mid-Shore counties are reporting rates between 22.9 percent and 38.3 percent. This represents a significant post-pandemic challenge affecting student learning and district operations. Reporters should investigate what districts are doing to address absenteeism, what barriers students face to regular attendance, and what support services exist for chronically absent students.

## Federal Immigration Policy and School Climate

Federal immigration policy shifts are creating new challenges for Shore schools. In January 2025, Superintendent Pepukayi's letter to Talbot County families reaffirming school safety for all students regardless of immigration status came in response to the Trump administration's rescission of the 2018 "sensitive locations" policy. The letter's emphasis on support networks for undocumented and immigrant families signals how districts are building support systems in politically volatile moments. Reporters should track whether other Shore districts issue similar communications and investigate whether immigration enforcement activity is affecting enrollment or student attendance.

---

## Alternative Educational Models

The emergence of microschool and hybrid homeschooling options represents a new dimension of Shore education coverage. Beyond Words Tutoring, founded by special-education teacher Lindsey Parte, launched a hybrid homeschooling microschool in Stevensville beginning September 2024, serving 6-11-year-olds with blended schedules and low student-to-teacher ratios. Reporters should monitor whether these models are growing and what they reveal about parental satisfaction with traditional public schooling.
