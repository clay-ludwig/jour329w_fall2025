Lessons Learned
December 12, 2025

---

My understanding of LLMs, both in general and in the context of journalism, has changed pretty drastically because of this class.

Specifically, I've been able to see how truly "artificial" LLM intelligence is. Before coming to this class, I loosely understood that the models were "guessing" things and regurgitating information from their training data, but I didn't appreciate just how probablistic they actually are.

Several examples of this were shown at the start of our class, like the "name 20 turtle names" prompt, which almost always returns a list containing the names "Shelly" or "Shellby." This probabilistic behavior was also evident in the experiences we shared after our homework assignments, where the distribution of people who reported having good, "meh" or bad experiences with things like Copilot was always pretty evenly distributed.

I also was struck by the Allen AI website you showed us a few weeks ago, which showed a "trace" of the LLM's thinking process back to its original training data. The fact that the "LLM speak" we've come to recognize may (i.e. "it's not x, it's y") be rooted in things as mundane as Amazon advertisements was really eye-opening for me, since it shows just how irrational and unintelligent these systems are (despite how much we tend to anthropomorphize them).

I've read many debates online about whether intelligence can be "encoded" into language through LLMs. After this class, I sit squarely in the camp of people who think the answer to this question is "no."

But somewhat paradoxically, I've also gained an even greater appreciation that these LLMs exist at all. They've been undeniably useful for us this semester, and I've gotten more confident (especially in the last few weeks) that a beat book is possible with today's models.

Also, while I still don't believe LLMs should be used to write articles, I definitely think they are useful tools earlier in the reporting process. Even just the fact that I can use Copilot to write code for data scraping, analysis, etc. is powerful. There were several times this semester when I was blown away by the capabilities of Claude in Copilot, like the CNS beat book agent I was able to create within only a few hours.

Despite their flaws, I also appreciated the ways that LLMs can be used *within* code pipelines, to assist with things like metadata creation (and further processing of that metadata). The fact that we can build a natural language "understanding" layer into our deterministic code is pretty cool.

Seeing how other journalists like Dylan Freedman used AI this semester was also really interesting. It was clear to me that he didn't fully trust the models to make judgement calls, but he clearly sees them as valuable additions to his data reporting processes.

That lack of trust in them to make judgement calls is, in my opinion, their biggest weakness. While they've definitely gotten more powerful with each iteration (even within this semester, where we saw new Claude, Gemini and GPT models released), I just don't trust them to make really important decisions. It's hard to quantify exactly what they're missing, but they always have the problem of being 85% of the way toward solving a problem. On a broad level, I found that very frustrating this semester.

Given everything I learned, here's the advice I would give to other journalism students:
- Check the models' work. Not necessarily because it always needs to be perfect or correct, but because it helps you understand their "thought process" more thoroughly.
- Be strategic about how you prompt LLMs, particularly when dealing with large, wide-ranging corpuses of data like we did in this class. Writing the wrong example(s) in a prompt, for example, can cause the model to misbehave. Being thoughtful about how you tell the models instructions, like giving them role-playing scenarios, can really improve their output.
- Don't expect the models to make good judgement calls, particularly around topics or tasks that are ethically-fraught. For example, do not trust a model to fact-check *for* you, even if it can assist your fact-checking process.
- Think from the start about context window limits, especially when working in news archives. There are a thousand different directions you can shoot in to solve this problem, and in my experience, most are worth exploring. I was routinely surprised this semester at how certain methods of compacting/removing context worked (and how others didn't).
